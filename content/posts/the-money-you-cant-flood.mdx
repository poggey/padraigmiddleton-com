In 2025, DeepMind’s AlphaEvolve found a faster way to multiply certain matrices, beating a record held for 56 years.[^alphaevolve] And in September 2026, OpenAI claimed a solution to part of the Navier–Stokes equations, a 90-year-old maths problem, saying it managed to find it in just 88 hours.[^openai] No doubt there are countless other problems being solved now as I’m writing this. So my question is: can any financial market be broken by AI?

My first thought was Bitcoin. Famously, it’s mined by powerful computers searching for a correct code, and every code they find earns them bitcoin. So you’d imagine that the faster you find codes, the more bitcoin you get. But if this was the case, the market would’ve already been flooded. Mining power has already grown trillions of times since Bitcoin started.[^hashrate]

The reason is that Bitcoin adjusts itself. Roughly every two weeks, the network checks how fast codes are being found; too fast and the network makes the codes harder to find, too slow and it makes them easier. It’s a self-regulating system, aiming for one code every 10 minutes: the golden rate.[^difficulty] Imagine it as a thermostat: you set the temperature, and it adjusts the heating to stay at a steady 20 degrees. At the moment, every code found earns 3.125 bitcoin, but this halves every four years, which is why the total is capped at 21 million.[^supply]

Say AI made a miner 1,000 times faster overnight. The network only adjusts after every 2,016 codes, which usually takes two weeks, but now takes about 20 minutes. And at any one adjustment, the codes can only get 4 times harder, so it takes 5 adjustments to catch up (4×4×4×4×4 = 1,024, just beating the increase in speed).[^cap] All of that takes about four or five days, and around 31,000 extra bitcoin get mined early. That sounds like a lot, but it’s roughly 0.16% of all bitcoin. The 21 million cap still remains, so all it means is that bitcoin due over the next ten weeks arrives this week instead.

Most things used as money can be flooded. Take silver, for example. When the Spanish found a mountain full of it in Bolivia, they mined it on an enormous scale and flooded Europe with it. Since silver was money, there was suddenly far more money but no more goods to spend it on. Each coin bought less, so prices rose, and kept rising for about a century.[^silver]

Bitcoin is different. Finding the equivalent of a silver mountain doesn’t give you a glut of bitcoin, it just makes the mountain harder to mine. In economic terms, its supply is perfectly inelastic: it doesn’t respond to price or cost at all. That makes it a currency that can’t be flooded, something almost no other money in history can claim.

But this breakthrough is also Bitcoin’s flaw. Its price is incredibly volatile, and a big reason is that fixed supply. When demand for most things rises, more gets produced and the price settles. Bitcoin can’t do that, so any change in demand feeds straight into its price.

There is a way to break the market, though. I simplified earlier when I said finding a code gets you bitcoin. In reality, it gives you the right to write the next page in Bitcoin’s shared record of payments. Normally, the person writing changes almost every time, so nobody is in control. But the miner from earlier (1,000 times faster) would find almost every code, giving them near-total control of the record. They could block other people’s payments or undo their own, and once the record can’t be trusted, Bitcoin is close to worthless.[^whitepaper]

So mining faster doesn’t break Bitcoin, unless one miner is so far ahead of everyone else that its advantage lasts, giving it control of every future page in the record. But this only changes my question: if someone had already found a way to mine 1,000 times faster, why would they use it? It would mean a brief period of gain, followed by a collapse in confidence and Bitcoin losing its value, including all the bitcoin they’d just mined. The smarter move would be to use only a fraction of it, taking a share of the rewards and never touching the record. And if someone is already doing that, would anyone even notice?
